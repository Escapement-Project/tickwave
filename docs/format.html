The tickwave recording format, version 1.0

A tickwave recording holds digital and analog channels on one time axis, exactly as the instruments recorded them: one capture's, or several captures merged, each channel in the ticks of the instrument that recorded it. Each channel is a stream of its own on that axis: its blocks, its overview and its index cost what its own data costs. A writer only ever appends to the file, so a recorder can stream it out while it captures. The file ends with a small footer. A reader with random access reads the end first and then only the channels and spans a view shows, so a page can show a few microseconds of one channel of a long capture without downloading the rest. A reader that takes the file in order needs no footer. A file cut off at any byte, by a crash, a power cut or a full disk, still reads up to its last whole record, and so does a file a recorder is still writing.

Status: draft. Version 1 is frozen when the first recording is published; files made before then may not read.

Times#

Every time a channel stores is in ticks of its source. A source's tick is its resolution: 1 ns for a 1 GS/s logic analyser, one sample period for a sigrok capture, the timescale for VCD, one count of a probe's stamping clock. Each source states its tick rate as an exact ratio. An edge, a gap's ends and the tick an analog run starts at are whole ticks. An analog channel's samples sit on a grid of their own: its run's start, plus the run's phase, a fixed fraction of a tick, plus a whole number of periods, and a period can be a ratio (see Header). A channel holds its source's times as the source gave them, so nothing in it is finer than the source stated, and no reading shows more resolution than the source had.

A source's time t, in its own seconds, is at source tick k = round((t − begin_s) × rate), where begin_s is the source's own time at its tick 0 and rate its tick rate. A converter refuses a source whose edges sit more than 0.01 of a tick off that grid, or whose samples sit more than that off their own. Every source tick a channel stores lies in 0..2^53.

The channels share one time axis, which ticks at the recording's timebase. Blocks, the overview, annotations and the length are in the axis's ticks. Channel c of source s holds source tick k at the axis's tick

T = origin_s + floor((k + offset_c) × m_s),   m_s = stretch_s × R / rate_s

R is the recording's timebase and rate_s the source's. origin_s is the source's origin, an axis tick. offset_c is the channel's offset, in its source's ticks, 0 where it states none. stretch_s is the source's stretch, 1 where it states none (see Sources). m_s is how many axis ticks one of the source's ticks lasts. It is an exact ratio, and positive. In lowest terms it is a/b, both below 2^53, and the mapping is integer arithmetic:

T = origin_s + floor((k + offset_c) × a / b)

floor rounds toward −∞: floor(−7/2) is −4. The product (k + offset_c) × a has a magnitude below 2^107, so a reader computes it in 128-bit integers. In JavaScript that is BigInt, whose division rounds toward 0: where the product is negative and leaves a remainder, take 1 off the quotient, since -1n / 2n is 0n and floor(−1/2) is −1. A later source tick never maps to an earlier axis tick, so a channel's items keep their order on the axis. Where m_s is below 1, two source ticks can share an axis tick. A reader refuses a source whose m_s is below 1/2: a source's own rate puts its m at 1 or more (see below), and its stretch lies in 1/2..=2, so at most two of its ticks share an axis tick.

A position that isn't a whole tick, an analog sample's place, maps by the same rule: k is then an exact ratio, and floor takes the whole axis tick at or before it. Every such place is a whole number of 1/D ticks, where D is its channel's grid (see Header), so a reader multiplies through by D and still computes in 128-bit integers: T = origin_s + floor((k × D + offset_c × D) × a / (b × D)).

m_s is (stretch num × R num × rate den) / (stretch den × R den × rate num). To find a and b, a reader takes each of the nine pairs of a term on top and a term below, and divides both by their greatest common divisor, before multiplying. The two products are then in lowest terms, and a reader refuses a source whose a or b reaches 2^53. Three terms below 2^53 can multiply past 128 bits, so a reader multiplies with an overflow check: a product that overflows has passed 2^53.

A recording of one source ticks at the source's rate, so its m is its stretch, and without one T = k + origin + offset. A reader refuses a recording of one source whose timebase has another value than the source's. A recording of several sources ticks at the least common multiple of their rates: the slowest rate at which each source's tick is a whole number of axis ticks, lcm(p_i) / gcd(q_i) for rates p_i/q_i in lowest terms. Each source without a stretch then has a whole-number m. Where that rate would put a term of the timebase, a source's m or the length at 2^53 or past it, the recording ticks at the fastest of the sources' rates instead, and a slower source's m is a ratio. A stretch multiplies its source's m, whatever the rate. A reader maps a recording of several sources by the timebase it states, whichever rate that is.

Every term of the mapping is an integer or an exact ratio in the header, so a script maps a source's ticks to the axis exactly. A channel stores its source's ticks, so a check against the source compares them directly. A recording converted whole has the source's timebase, an origin of 0 and no offset or stretch: T = k, and it starts where the source does.

Layout#

All numbers are little-endian, and an f32 is an IEEE 754 binary32.

Part Size Holds
preamble 12 bytes magic and version
records up to the trailer the declaration, then blocks, known-through records, gaps, runs, overview blocks, pages, annotations and annotation parts as they come, then the annotation bundle and the footer
trailer 24 bytes where the footer is

A finished file ends with the footer record and the trailer. A file without them was cut off or is still being written (see Reading in order, and Repairing a cut-off file).

Preamble#

Offset Type Field
0 8 bytes magic: 89 54 4b 57 0d 0a 1a 0a (\x89TKW\r\n\x1a\n)
8 u16 major version, 1
10 u16 minor version, 0

A minor version adds header fields that older readers ignore, optional records (see Records), which they skip, and channel kinds, which they show as unavailable (see Header). It adds only what an older reader can leave out and still read everything else as the writer meant: a field whose absence changes nothing else's meaning, a record nothing else depends on, a channel no stored channel needs. A field or record it adds is never content: a reader of an earlier minor version computes the content id without it (see Content id). A channel of a kind it adds is content that an earlier reader can't hash, as with a channel whose codec or requires it doesn't know: its full check refuses the recording. A change to how existing data reads goes behind a feature name in requires (see Header), which a reader that doesn't know the name refuses, for the recording or for one channel. Any other change is a major version: it changes the layout, and a reader refuses a major version it does not know.

A tool that writes a recording again from one it reads, as re-cutting, repair, merging and bundling do, writes only what its own version defines. So it refuses an input of a later minor version than its own, and one holding an optional record of a kind it doesn't know: it would leave out what that version adds while the content id stayed the same. It drops every auxiliary record.

The declaration is the first record, and only the first. The footer is the last, and the trailer follows it. The rules below say where the others go.

Kind 0 is never a record, so a tail of zeros, which a power cut can leave, stops a reader. A reader refuses a record of a kind it does not know from 1 to 127, kind 9 included. Kinds from 128 up are for later versions, and a reader skips one it doesn't know:

  • 128 to 191, optional records: what a later minor version adds. A tool that writes the recording again refuses one it doesn't know (see Preamble).
  • 192 to 255, auxiliary records: only what can be computed again from the rest of the file, such as a cache or an index. An auxiliary record is not content: no content id covers it, and a full check doesn't check it. A reader that knows its kind and finds it doesn't fit the file ignores it rather than refusing the file. A tool that writes the recording again drops it. A writer may add one without raising the minor version. The header's auxiliary says where a later version's auxiliary records are, for a reader with random access.

A record from 128 up goes after the declaration and before the annotation bundle, or before the footer in a file without one. A file whose first record is not the declaration is refused, whatever that record's kind.

A reader that reads the preamble too, in order or to repair, refuses a trailer whose versions differ from it.

Declaration#

UTF-8 JSON: the header's timebase, channels, derived, sources, title, meta, requires and overview_shift, as far as a writer knows them before any data.

A channel entry has the header's name, source_name, source and kind, and offset, offset_kind, kept, significant, overview_shift, codec and requires where it states them. A digital channel has initial, and an analog channel unit, period, coding and phase where it states one. What only the end settles is left out: a digital channel's end, edges and gaps, an analog channel's first, count, breaks and losses, and every channel's blocks and overview_blocks. A writer that does not know a level at the channel's start (see Header) declares it false and starts the channel with a gap from its start.

The footer's header holds the same values for every field this version defines. It may add keys to a source's extra, for what the source states only at its end, such as a probe's totals. A declaration doesn't state auxiliary. A reader reads a declaration that states length, content_id, annotation_parts, auxiliary or what only the end settles as if it didn't.

Header#

The footer's header is UTF-8 JSON. Every integer in it is below 2^53, and every number fits an f64, so a JavaScript reader parses it exactly. No object in it names a key twice, at any depth, meta and extra included, since a parser that keeps the last would read another value than one that keeps the first. The same holds for the declaration. A reader refuses either where a number or an object breaks this.

Field Type Meaning
timebase [num, den] the axis's ticks a second (see Times)
length integer how many axis ticks the capture covers: ticks 0..length
channels array one entry a channel, in channel order
derived array, optional channels computed from others, not stored in blocks (see Derived channels)
sources array where the channels came from, one entry a source (see Sources)
title string, optional
meta object of strings, optional free keys, such as probe or firmware
content_id string blake3: and the 64 lower-case hex digits of the content's digest (below)
overview_shift integer the finest overview level's shift of a channel that states none (see Overview)
requires array of strings, optional features a reader must understand to read the recording at all
annotation_parts integer, optional the number of annotation parts, at least 1, absent where there are none (see Annotations)
auxiliary object, optional where a later version's auxiliary records are (see Records); this version defines no key in it

A channel entry has name, source_name (the source's own name for it), source (its source's index in sources) and kind, and offset, offset_kind and kept where it states them (see Moved and trimmed channels). It can state overview_shift, its finest overview level's shift. It has blocks, the number of its blocks, and overview_blocks where any of its overview levels go in overview blocks: one {"shift": s, "blocks": b, "rows": r} a level, finest first, with b the level's overview blocks and r its index rows (see Overview and Index). An analog channel can state significant, an integer from 1 to 17: its readings carry at most that many significant figures, as an instrument whose step changes with its range does.

requires names features a later version adds that change how data reads. This version defines none, so its writers state none. A reader refuses a recording whose requires names one it doesn't know. A channel entry, and a derived entry, can state requires too: a reader that doesn't know a name there reads the recording without that channel. It shows the channel as unavailable, by name, skips its blocks, overview and strip, shows every derived channel over it as unavailable too, and leaves out every annotation that names it, saying which (see Unavailable channels). A channel entry can state codec, how its blocks are stored. This version defines none, and a channel without one stores its blocks as Blocks says. A reader treats a channel with a codec it doesn't know as unavailable. No requires, codec or operation changes a channel's entries in the top index or the headers of its records, so a reader finds and skips an unavailable channel's tables and records by their layout alone. Nor does one change the JSON type of a field this version defines, so a reader that doesn't know it still parses the entry.

  • "digital": initial (the level at the channel's start before any edge there, a boolean), end (a source tick: the level is known from the channel's start to end; a source can stop knowing one channel before another), edges (the count), and its gaps where the level was not known for a while (see Gaps, below). A channel's start is its kept span's from (see Moved and trimmed channels). In a recording that states no kept span it is the source tick at the axis's tick 0, −origin. An end at the channel's start says the level is never known. Such a channel states 0 edges and no gaps, and its initial is its declaration's, which says nothing of the level. A reader refuses one that states others before it reads a block.
  • "analog": unit (empty when the source states none), first (the tick its first run starts at, 0 when there is no sample), phase ([num, den], with num from 1 to den − 1, absent when 0), period (source ticks from one sample to the next, [num, den]), count, its breaks where the samples stopped for a while and its losses at either end (see Gaps), and coding (see Codings).

A channel's kind can be one a later version defines. A reader shows a channel of a kind it doesn't know as unavailable, as it does one whose requires it doesn't know. Such an entry has name, source_name, source, kind, blocks and overview_blocks, as every channel's does, its offset, offset_kind and kept where it states them (see Moved and trimmed channels), and the overview_shift and requires its version gives it. A reader reads only those, and checks them, its source and its levels; it never parses the entry's other keys, whatever their type. Its tables in the top index are its blocks and its overview levels, in that order. Its block records keep the head Blocks gives, and a reader places them without decoding them. It has no gap or run record, and a reader refuses one that names it. Any other record or table of the channel is of a kind from 128 up.

An analog channel's samples come in runs, period apart, each run starting a fraction of a tick, its phase, after a whole tick. A channel without breaks has one run, and its sample i sits at source tick first + phase + i × period exactly, its place, and on the axis at the tick its place maps to. An instrument whose analog samples sit at a fixed offset from its digital channels' ticks, smaller than a tick, states it as the phase. A phase is not a move: a move is a claim about the channel's path (see Moved and trimmed channels), and a phase is where the instrument sampled.

A channel's grid D is the least common multiple of the denominators of its phase and its period, in lowest terms, 1 where both are whole. Every place of the channel is a whole number of 1/D ticks, and so is every break's phase (see Gaps).

Every axis tick the header and the index name lies in 0..length: every block's start. An annotation part's row is the exception, since its part may be written before the length is known (see Index). Every source tick a channel names lies in 0..2^53 and maps into 0..length (see Times): every edge and every sample's place, every tick a gap or a break names, the start of a loss before the first sample, and a kept span's from. A digital channel's end, a kept span's to, and the end of a loss after the last sample map into 0..=length. An analog channel has no end: its samples may stop before the length. In a recording that states no kept span, the source's origin is at most 0, so each channel's start maps to tick 0. length is at least 1. It is below 2^53, and so are the terms of every timebase, period and stretch, the terms of each source's m in lowest terms, and every edges, count, blocks, rows, break index and lost count. Every origin, align and offset has a magnitude below 2^53. No term of a timebase, a period or a stretch is zero. A period is at least 1, so no two samples of a run share a source tick. A stretch lies in 1/2..=2. A channel's grid D is below 2^16. Every [num, den] in the header, a coding's scale and offset included, is in lowest terms with a positive den, so one value has one spelling and one content id. A reader refuses a header that breaks any of these before it reads a block.

Sources#

sources lists where the channels came from. A recording converted from one capture has one entry; a merged one has an entry for each source of each recording merged into it. Every entry is some channel's source. A merged recording takes each entry from the recording merged into it, with origin and align restated on its own axis, stretch multiplied by any the merge states for that recording, and every other field kept. A value x on that recording's axis is restated as floor(x × s × R_merged / R_input), where s is the stretch the merge states for it, 1 where it states none. The origin then moves by that recording's alignment and by where the merged recording starts, and the align by that recording's alignment. Its channels keep the source ticks they had there, so a check against that recording compares them directly, and maps the header's values by the same rule.

An entry has:

  • format: saleae-binary-0, saleae-binary-1, sigrok-sr or vcd for a capture tickwave converts, or the name a recorder gives its own stream. A reader keeps a name it doesn't know.
  • files: an array of names, no directories. A recorder's own stream reads no file, and lists none: [].
  • timebase: the source's tick rate, [num, den]. It can be any exact ratio: a recorder that measured its clock against a reference states the rate it measured. One of its ticks lasts m axis ticks (see Times).
  • origin: an integer, the axis tick where the source's tick 0 lies before any channel's offset (see Times). Every move, cut and alignment of the source's channels folds into it.
  • Optionally align: axis ticks that whoever merged it moved this source by against the others, positive later, to line up instruments that saw the same event. Like offset, it is a claim, not something an instrument recorded. Instruments' clocks drift: at 20 ppm apart, an alignment by hand stays within one tick of a 96 MHz clock for about ±0.5 ms around the point where it was made, and within 10 µs for about ±0.5 s.
  • Optionally stretch: [num, den], how much whoever merged it lengthened this source's ticks against its timebase, to line up an instrument whose clock ran at another rate than it states. The source's tick 0 stays at origin, and on the axis its tick k lies k × stretch / rate seconds after it, before the mapping's floor. A stretch above 1 says the source's clock ran slow. Like align, it is a claim. Two events both instruments saw give both: align from one, and stretch from the time between them. What a stretch leaves is the clocks' wander. 1 where it states none.
  • Optionally title and meta: the title and metadata of the recording a merge took the source from, since the merged recording's own title and meta are the merge's. A source that came from a merge already keeps those of its own recording.
  • Optionally instrument, software, begin_s (the source's own time at its tick 0, in seconds), trigger_s (the trigger's time in the same seconds), rate, extra (anything else the source states) and converter. begin_s and trigger_s are numbers, extra an object of strings, and the others strings. rate is there when the source doesn't record its sample rate: inferred when the converter took the lowest rate on whose grid every edge sits, given when the rate was given to it.

A reading of one channel keeps to its source's tick, and a reading between two sources' channels, an alignment's included, to the coarser of the two. A reading tied to no channel keeps to the finest source's tick. On the axis a source's tick lasts m ticks, not always a whole number. A reading of times within one channel takes them from its source ticks, which the file holds exactly, rather than from the axis ticks they map to.

Codings#

An analog channel's coding says how its samples are stored:

  • {"type": "f32"} and {"type": "f64"}: the source's floats.
  • {"type": "int", "bits": b, "signed": s}, with b in 1..=64 and s a boolean: integer samples, each a value exactly. A signed sample lies in −2^(b−1)..2^(b−1), an unsigned one in 0..2^b, and a reader refuses one outside. It can also state "scale": [num, den] and "offset": [num, den], each an exact ratio with a positive denominator, a nonzero scale and terms whose magnitude is below 2^53. A sample's value is then offset + code × scale exactly: an ADC's counts, stored as the instrument gave them, with the factor that makes them volts or amps. One code step, scale, is the finest a reading of it shows. A reader that needs the value as an f64 takes the code as an f64, multiplies by the scale's numerator, divides by its denominator, and adds the offset's numerator divided by its denominator: each step in f64, rounded once, so every reader gets the same float. Without an offset, and with code × num below 2^53, that float is the one nearest the value. In JavaScript, (code * num) / den + onum / oden. A coding without an offset adds nothing.
  • {"type": "codes", "bits": b, "scale": s, "offset": o}, with b in 1..=16 and s and o JSON numbers, each read as the f64 nearest it: a smaller store for an f32 source. A sample's value is code × s in f64, then o + that in f64, then rounded to f32: two separately rounded f64 operations, never a fused multiply-add, which can round to a different f32. In JavaScript, Math.fround(o + code * s). A converter picks this coding only after checking that every sample reproduces its source value bit for bit.

A reading of an f32 or f64 channel shows four significant figures unless the channel states significant. A reading of an int channel keeps to one code step and to significant where stated, whichever is coarser.

Derived channels#

A derived channel is computed from two analog channels, so a reader draws it without the recording storing its samples. derived holds one entry each: {"name": n, "unit": u, "op": o, "operands": [a, b]}, where a and b name analog channels, in order, and may name one twice, and o is the operation. unit is empty where the entry states none. No two channels or derived channels share a name, and a reader refuses a header where any do. Its operands come from one source, and share first, phase, period, count, breaks, their losses at either end and their move, the channel's offset (0 where a channel states none), so its sample i sits where theirs does. A reader refuses operands that differ in any of these, rather than pair samples from two grids. It compares what the header holds when it opens the recording, and the operands' list pages wherever it fetches both, so it needn't fetch a list of breaks to draw a span: the first operand's breaks are the derived channel's.

This version defines two operations:

  • "multiply", a_i × b_i, as power is current times voltage. When both are int channels whose codings state no offset, their bits sum to at most 64, and the magnitudes of their scales' numerators multiply to below 2^53 and so do their denominators, sample i is the integer product of their codes, signed if either is, with scale the product of their scales, exactly: their numerators' product over their denominators' product, not reduced, which its f64 follows. Otherwise it is f64(a_i) × f64(b_i), rounded once.
  • "subtract", a_i − b_i, of their values, as a differential probe's two inputs give one signal. For two int channels, let s be the greatest common divisor of their scales' magnitudes, as ratios: gcd(|p| × v, |u| × q) / (q × v) for scales p/q and u/v in lowest terms, itself in lowest terms. ka = scale_a / s and kb = scale_b / s are then whole numbers. The result is the code ka × code_a − kb × code_b, signed, with scale s and offset offset_a − offset_b (an offset a channel doesn't state is 0), and with bits the smallest that holds that code for every pair of codes the operands' codings allow. It is that integer where its bits is at most 64 and every term of s and of the offset has a magnitude below 2^53. A reader that finds the code's range passing 128 bits takes the result as f64, since its bits passes 64. Otherwise, and for any other operands, sample i is f64(a_i) − f64(b_i), rounded once.

In both, a scale a channel doesn't state is 1.

An int operand's f64 follows the rule for an int channel. An integer result's value as an f64 follows that rule too, and a reading of it the rule for an int channel; a reading of an f64 result follows the rule for a float. A derived entry can state significant, as an analog channel can.

A later version can add operations, and an operation's further terms go in params, an object of strings, so a reader that doesn't know the operation can still check the content id (see Content id). The operations this version defines take no params, and a reader treats an entry of one of them that gives some as unavailable, as it does an entry whose op it doesn't know (see Unavailable channels). For such an entry it checks only that its name is unique, that it names at least one operand and each names a channel, and that params, where stated, is an object of strings. It can still find the channel's tables and strip, since a derived channel's finest level follows from its operands' whatever its operation (see Overview), and skip them.

Blocks hold nothing for a derived channel. It has an overview of its own, so a reader draws it zoomed out from the overview alone, and measures a span decoding only the operands' blocks at the span's two ends. Its entry has overview_blocks as a channel's does. Derived channels are numbered after the channels: derived channel d is overview channel C + d, where C is the number of channels.

Moved and trimmed channels#

Whoever converts a capture can move a channel along the time axis by a whole number of its source's ticks, to correct a known delay in its path, and keep only part of a channel. Neither is what the instrument recorded, so the recording states them: a moved channel its offset, and, once any channel is moved or cut, every channel the span that holds its source data, kept. A recording with neither is as the source recorded it.

  • offset: the source ticks the channel was moved by, an integer; positive is later (see Times for how it maps). A converter refuses an offset more than 0.01 of the source's tick off its grid.
  • offset_kind: why it was moved, stated with every offset. "delay" is a known delay in the channel's path, such as a probe's lead or an instrument's input. A reader keeps a kind it doesn't know, and refuses an offset without one, or one without an offset.
  • kept: [from, to], the source ticks from..to where the channel holds what the source recorded; to is not included. They are ticks as the channel stores them, before its move. On the axis the span runs from the tick from maps to up to the tick to maps to. Outside it the channel holds nothing from the source: it was cut, or the source had not started or had stopped. Neither is a loss by the instrument. Every channel states kept when any channel is moved or cut, when the recording has several sources, or when its source states a stretch other than 1, and none does otherwise. A reader refuses a recording where only some channels state it, or where all do and none starts at the axis's tick 0: no channel's from maps to 0. A digital channel starts at its from: its edges and gaps lie in from..end, and its end is at most to. A cut inside a gap the instrument left starts the channel with a gap from from, and the instrument can stop before the cut does. A reader shows the axis outside the kept span as outside the source's data, and a gap as lost. A digital channel known nowhere in its kept span has its end at from and no gap, as any channel never known has. An analog channel's samples lie inside: its first is at least from, and its last sample's place is below to. A reader refuses a kept that its channel's data contradicts.

A cut keeps every edge in its span, one at from included, and the channel's initial is its level just before from. It keeps every analog sample whose place lies in the span, and each keeps its place: a run that the cut starts inside starts at its first kept sample, at that sample's place, its whole tick as the run's tick and the rest as its phase. Nothing moves forward, and no kept sample is lost. That phase is the channel's declared one, so it sets the grid, and a later run's phase has to be a whole number of the new grid's units. A converter refuses a cut where it isn't, which only a run whose phase has a finer denominator than the period's and the cut's first phase can meet.

When no channel is moved or cut, and there is one source with no stretch, the recording starts at the source's tick −origin: where the source does, for a capture converted whole. Otherwise it starts at the earliest of the channels' starts on the axis, each after its move and its source's alignment and stretch: a channel starts at the tick its kept span's from maps to.

Gaps#

A live recorder can lose data or pause. Its channels then have gaps: spans where nothing is known. The header says where they are. A sidecar annotation can say why one happened.

A digital channel's gaps are a list of [from, to, level], in its source's ticks. The level is not known at source ticks from..to. From to on it is level, a boolean, until the next edge. No edge lies in from..=to. An edge at to would leave the level there ambiguous, so a writer folds it into level. An edge at from means the level there is known, so the gap starts after it. The gaps go in time order: start ≤ from < to < end, where start is the channel's start (see Header), and each gap's from lies past the to before it. A channel with a gap from its start has an initial of false, since its level there is not known.

An analog channel's breaks are a list of [index, tick, lost] or [index, tick, lost, phase]: sample index starts a new run, at source tick tick plus phase / D, and the instrument lost lost samples before it, 0 when it only paused. phase is a whole number in 0..D, where D is the channel's grid, and 0 where the entry has three terms. The first run starts at [0, first], at the channel's phase. Sample i of the run that starts at [index, tick] with phase f sits at source tick tick + f / D + (i − index) × period exactly, its place on the run's grid. The grid goes on past the run's last sample, and before the first run's first sample. Each index lies in 1..count and is larger than the one before it. Each run's start is at least the place of the sample before it, and where it states lost samples, past place index on the run before it, so at least part of a lost sample lies before it. A run on an instrument whose sampling clock kept running through a pause starts on the old run's grid, and with a period that isn't whole, its phase can differ from the run before it. A reader draws no line across a break. The lost samples sit where the run before the break would have put them: their time runs from place index on that run's grid to place index + lost, and ends at the new run's start if that comes sooner. The rest of the break is a pause. A reader shows the two apart.

An analog channel can also state samples the instrument lost before its first sample and after its last, each at least 1, and absent when none:

  • lost_before: they sit at places −lost_before to −1 on the first run's grid, so their time runs from the place of −lost_before to the first sample's place. A reader refuses a loss whose start lies below source tick 0 or maps before axis tick 0.
  • lost_after: they sit at places count to count + lost_after − 1 on the last run's grid, so their time runs from the place of count to the place of count + lost_after. A reader refuses a loss whose end maps past the length, and a writer refuses a length that doesn't cover it.

A reader pales those spans as it pales lost samples inside, and statistics count them as not measured.

Each sample's time runs from its place to the next place on its run's grid, so inside a run it reaches the next sample's place, and every sample of a run lasts one period. A run's last sample ends at the earliest of the next place on its grid, the next run's start, its channel's kept to where it states one, and the source time the length maps back to (below). A lost span is cut the same way. These are exact times. All but the last are whole numbers of 1/D source ticks.

On the axis a sample covers the ticks from the one its place maps to up to the one its end maps to, which is what a reader draws. A reader that measures a span of the axis measures in exact time instead, before any floor. The span's ends map back to source time exactly: axis tick T is source time (T − origin_s) / m_s − offset_c. Each sample weighs the length of its time inside the span, times m_s, so a stretch counts and the floor onto the axis changes nothing. A sample whose time reaches into the span counts even when its place lies before it. So a measurement over two spans side by side adds up to the measurement over both. With integer codes it is exact, in exact rationals: with a stretch or a grid finer than a tick, its sums pass 128 bits.

A list of at most 64 entries is in the header: a digital channel's gaps and an analog channel's breaks, each an array of the entries above. A longer list is in list pages (see Index), and the header states its length instead, as gap_count or break_count. A channel with no gap or break states neither, or states an empty list. A reader refuses a list in the header of more than 64 entries, and a gap_count or break_count of 64 or fewer.

Blocks#

A channel's items are cut by time into blocks of its own: a block holds exactly the channel's edges or samples whose axis ticks lie in its span. A channel's blocks tile the axis in order: the first starts at tick 0, each later one at the end of the one before, every one ends after it starts, and the last ends at the length. Each channel's blocks are cut apart from every other channel's, so a slow channel's block can span hours while a fast one's spans milliseconds.

A writer closes a channel's block once its decoded size reaches a target. It never closes one between two of the channel's items at the same axis tick. A writer may also close a block before it reaches the target, at an axis tick before which it has every item of the channel. A live recorder does this so a crash loses little, and such a block can hold no item at all. A block decodes to at most 4 MiB (see Limits), so a writer's target leaves room for the items it takes before it can close.

A block record's payload:

Offset Type Field
0 u32 channel
4 i64 start, an axis tick
12 i64 end, the channel's next block's start
20 u32 the channel's word, as the index has it
24 u32 decoded length
28 the rest the block: one zstd frame

The word: for a digital channel, its edge count in the block, with the level at the block's start in bit 31; for an analog channel, its sample count in the block. A digital channel's level bit is initial in its block 0. In each later block it is the bit before it, taken through the earlier block's edges and gap ends in the order of their source ticks: an edge flips it, and a gap's to sets it to the gap's level. A gap ends in the channel's block that holds the axis tick its to maps to. Inside a gap, the bit carries the level from before the gap, and before the channel's start it carries initial.

Each block, overview block, strip, annotation part and annotation bundle is one standard zstd frame with a content checksum, and nothing else: no skippable frame, no second frame, no byte after it, and no dictionary. A reader refuses a frame without a checksum, with one that does not match, or with anything else in its bytes. (A decoder that reads every frame it is given would check only the last checksum, so a valid empty frame after a corrupted block would hide the corruption.)

A block's decoded payload is its channel's section, laid out as below for a channel that states no codec:

  • Digital. Nothing when the block holds no edge. Otherwise unsigned LEB128 numbers: step, at least 1; the first edge's source tick; then each later edge's interval, its source ticks from the edge before it, divided by step. A writer takes step as the greatest common divisor of the block's intervals that are not zero, and 1 when there is none. A source declared at a multiple of its rate stamps only multiples of some tick count, so step takes that factor out, and its blocks come out about the size they have at its own rate. An interval may be zero: two edges at one tick are a pulse the source stamped with a single time, and the level flips twice there. Every edge lies in start..end of its channel, and the axis tick it maps to lies in the block's span. An edge at the channel's start flips the level from initial, as any edge does, so a pulse at the capture's first instant keeps both its edges. A reader refuses a step of 0, an edge that lies outside those bounds, and bytes left after the last edge.
  • Analog. The block's samples in the channel's coding. f32 and f64 values are stored whole. Codes, of an int or a codes channel, are stored in byte planes: every code's lowest byte, then every code's next byte, and so on for ceil(bits / 8) bytes, a signed code in two's complement. Split this way, the captures we tried came out 12 % smaller after zstd than with each code's bytes together. A reader refuses a code outside its coding's range.

Known-through records#

A known-through record's payload is one i64, tick: every item of every channel before axis tick tick is in a block record before this record. So a channel whose last block ends before tick holds no item from that end to tick. A gap is not an item: a gap record after a known-through record may start before its tick, as a recorder that reports a loss when it ends writes one, but its to maps to that tick or after it, since the overview before it may already be written. Each known-through record's tick is later than the one before it, and no later than the length. Every block record before it ends at or before its tick, so a file cut just after it holds nothing past its tick (see Repairing a cut-off file). A reader refuses a known-through record that a block record before it ends after, a block record after it that holds an item before its tick, and a gap record after it whose to maps before its tick.

A live recorder writes one at each flush, after that flush's block records (see Writing), so a channel with nothing new needs no record of its own, and repair cuts every channel to the same tick. A flush at the tick of the flush before it writes none.

Gap and run records#

A gap record:

Offset Type Field
0 u32 channel
4 i64 from, a source tick
12 i64 to, a source tick
20 u8 level, 0 or 1

A digital channel's gap, as Gaps defines one. A channel's gap records come in time order: each one's from lies past the to of the one before it, and no edge of the channel lies in its from..=to. It comes before the channel's block that holds the axis tick to maps to. The channel's blocks before that one may already be written when the writer learns of the gap. They stay as they are, because a block's level bit carries the level from before a gap until the gap's to. A reader refuses a gap record out of order, one that an edge contradicts, and one whose to maps before the end of the channel's last block record before it.

A run record:

Offset Type Field
0 u32 channel
4 u64 index
12 i64 tick, a source tick
20 u64 lost
28 u32 phase, in 1/D ticks: below D, the channel's grid

Sample index of an analog channel starts a run at tick plus phase / D, after lost samples the instrument lost. The run at index 0 gives the channel's first, its phase is the declared phase, and its lost is the channel's lost_before (see Gaps); each later run is a break, with its phase. A channel's run records come in order of index, the first at 0 and each later one larger than the one before it, and each starts where Gaps says a run may. It comes before the channel's block that holds sample index. A reader refuses a run record out of order, one with a phase of D or more, one at index 0 whose phase isn't the declared one, one that starts where Gaps says a run may not, and one whose index is below the samples the channel's block records before it hold.

The header's gaps and breaks are these records, in the order they came, but for the ones left out. A gap whose to maps at or past the length is left out of the header, and the channel's end becomes the gap's from if that comes sooner: a recorder that stops inside a loss reports the loss up to where it stopped. A gap from the channel's start then leaves its end at its start: the level is never known. Every other gap is listed, and ends before its channel's end: a reader refuses a gap record whose to is at or past its channel's end and maps before the length. A run whose index is not below its channel's count is left out too.

So a writer can't know that a channel's last gap or run is listed until another comes, the gap's block closes or the run's first sample comes, or it finishes. One that hashes the lists as they come (see Content id) holds back what it can't yet know. A page of a gap list comes after the channel's block that holds the axis tick of its last gap's to, and a page of a break list after the block that holds its last break's sample, so no page written holds an entry that is left out.

Overview#

The overview lets a reader draw a view without decoding blocks. Each channel and each derived channel has an overview of its own: levels of buckets of 2^shift axis ticks, each summarising that channel alone. A level has ceil(length / 2^shift) buckets. An item counts in the bucket that holds the axis tick it maps to.

A channel's finest level's shift is its overview_shift, or the recording's where it states none, at most 62. A derived channel's is the coarser of its operands'. A channel's levels go from it in steps of 4, stopping at the first with at most 1,024 buckets or at shift 62. That last level is the channel's strip, in the footer (see Footer). Every other level goes in overview blocks, which a reader fetches like blocks, for the span it shows. The levels follow from length and the shift, and a reader refuses a header that lists any others in overview_blocks.

A derived channel's finest level is its operands' coarser one whatever its operation, so a reader lays out every derived channel's tables without knowing its operation.

A writer declares each channel's finest level before it knows the length. tickwave's writers pick the shift that puts about 64 of the channel's items in a bucket: the whole number nearest log2(64 × t), held to 0..=62, where t is the channel's axis ticks an item. That is the s for which (64 × t)² lies between 2^(2s − 1) and 2^(2s + 1); no ratio falls on either bound. An analog channel's t is its period times its source's m. A digital channel's is the axis ticks from its start to its end, over its edge count. A channel with no item takes 62. A recorder that has no data yet takes the interval it expects between the channel's items, such as its sample period, and a channel whose rate it can't know ahead, such as a digital pin, takes the recording's shift. Since t is in axis ticks, the same data at a finer timebase gets a larger shift and about as many buckets. A view whose column is narrower than a channel's finest bucket reads the channel's items, about 64 a bucket.

A channel's level decodes to, for its n buckets:

  • Digital. n density bytes, then ceil(n / 2) duty bytes. Together they say how busy the channel is in each bucket, and whether it spends its time high or low there, for a view too coarse to draw edges. Neither is exact: the edges are in the blocks.

    A bucket's density is 0 when no edge falls in it, and otherwise 1 + floor(4 × log2(e)) for its e edges: one step every quarter of a doubling, so a bucket with twice as many edges is four steps up, at any zoom and in any recording. Counts are below 2^53, so a density is at most 212. Small counts are exact: 1, 5 and 7 are one, two and three edges. A gap's ends are not edges; two edges at one tick are two. A reader computes it in integers, since a floating-point log2 lands on the wrong side of a step for some counts below 2^53: with x = e << (63 − floor(log2 e)) as a u64, the density is 1 + 4 × floor(log2 e) plus one for each of 0x9837f0518db8a970, 0xb504f333f9de6485 and 0xd744fccad69d6af5 that x is at least: the thresholds ceil(2^(63 + r/4)) for r = 1, 2, 3.

    A bucket's duty is 4 bits. Of the bucket's axis ticks, K lie in the channel's kept span: at or after the tick its start maps to, and before the tick its kept to maps to, or before the length where it states no kept span. H of those are high: the level at a tick is initial taken through every edge and gap end at or before it, as the block words take it, so inside a gap, and past the channel's end, the level from before counts. The duty is 15 when K is 0, so 15 says the bucket lies wholly outside the channel's kept span, and otherwise 14 × H / K rounded to the nearest whole number, a half rounding up: floor((28 × H + K) / (2 × K)), in 128-bit integers. A bucket wholly low reads 0, and one wholly high 14. Bucket 2j is the low 4 bits of duty byte j and bucket 2j + 1 the high 4 bits; with n odd, the last byte's high 4 bits are 0.

    Duty takes no account of gaps or of the channel's end, because a recorder reports a loss when it ends: a gap can start in a bucket a writer has already written, and a loss still running when the recorder stops moves the channel's end back over such buckets. A reader shows gaps from the channel's gap list, and the span past its end as not known, instead.

    A bucket of the finest level counts its edges and ticks. A bucket of a coarser level takes the sums of the 16 buckets of the level below it that it covers, edges, K and H alike, and computes its density and duty from them, never from theirs, so every writer gives the same bytes. A writer keeps those sums while it builds; the file doesn't store them.

  • Analog. n minimum values, then n maximum values, then n sums of its samples: an int channel's codes, and every other channel's values. A sample counts in the bucket that holds the axis tick its place maps to.

    Coding Minimum and maximum Sum
    f32 f32 f64
    codes f32, of the values f64, of the values
    f64 f64 f64
    int, unsigned unsigned, w bytes i128
    int, signed two's complement, w bytes i128

    w is the smallest of 1, 2, 4 and 8 bytes that holds the coding's bits.

    A bucket with no sample holds a minimum above its maximum: +∞ and −∞, or the largest and the smallest number its w bytes hold. NaN samples are left out. Where −0 and +0 tie for a minimum or a maximum, the first in index order counts. A sum starts at +0, so an empty bucket's is 0. A bucket of the finest level sums its samples in index order, and a bucket of a coarser level sums the 16 buckets of the level below it that it covers, in order, so every writer gives the same bytes. An integer sum is exact. A bucket's sample count follows from first, phase, period, breaks and the mapping, so the overview doesn't store it.

  • Derived. As an analog channel, in the coding its operation gives (see Derived channels): an integer product as signed or unsigned int codes whose bits are its operands' summed, an integer difference as signed int codes of its bits, and an f64 result as f64.

A level in overview blocks is cut into blocks of 4,096 buckets, the last block the rest. An overview block record's payload:

Offset Type Field
0 u32 channel, a derived channel numbered after the channels
4 u8 the level's shift
5 u64 k: its first block starts at bucket 4,096 k
13 u32 n, at least 1
17 the rest one zstd frame

The frame decodes to one block's buckets, laid out as the channel's level for those buckets, and blocks k..k + n of the level are each those bytes. So a run of alike blocks, from a span with nothing in it or a signal that repeats, costs one record and one row. A level's last block, which may hold fewer buckets, is always a record of its own with n = 1, so a run's blocks are all whole. tickwave's writer writes each run of alike blocks as one record, up to n = 2^32 − 1. The records of one channel's level come in order of k. Those of different channels or different levels keep no order among themselves: a writer may settle one channel's levels before another's.

Annotations#

An annotation record's payload is a u16, the length of s, then s, then a. s is the generator's name in UTF-8, as a generator file's first line gives it, and a is one line of that file (ANNOTATIONS.md): a UTF-8 JSON object, ending with a newline and holding no other. Its times are axis ticks in 0..2^53, JSON integers, as a writer writes them: a time with a unit, as a hand-written file may give one, is not. A reader refuses a record whose s is not a name ANNOTATIONS.md allows a generator, since the source's file is named after it, and one whose a holds a newline before its end, is not a JSON object, or states a key twice. It refuses, too, a record whose line has no start, a start or end that is not such an integer, or an end before its start, since the row of the part covering it couldn't state its span. tickwave's writer refuses, at the call, an annotation whose fields, ticks or channels break ANNOTATIONS.md's rules for a line, and writes the rest as compact JSON. Its length aside, which the writer learns only when it finishes, each annotation it takes resolves. Another writer may write a line that breaks ANNOTATIONS.md's other rules. A re-cutter, repair and a merge leave out a line that breaks a rule on a key they know, naming it: a re-cutter wherever it lies, repair among the records it writes again after its cut. A key they don't know they carry, and name, since a later minor version of ANNOTATIONS.md may define it: a record's line comes before its generator's first line, which states that version and arrives with the bundle. A record before repair's cut stays where it is, since the part after it covers it. Annotations keep no order with the channels' items.

Annotation records let a live recorder keep what it generates as safely as its data. The footer does not index them. A file that holds them ends with them bundled, in annotation parts and an annotation bundle record, so a live recording stays one file, and a reader with random access reads them from there. A reader in order refuses a footer that comes after an annotation record no part covers, since a reader with random access would never see it.

An annotation part holds a run of one generator's lines. Its record's payload is a u32, the decoded length, then one zstd frame that decodes to a u16, the length of s, then s, the generator's name, then the lines, each ending with its newline. A part covers the annotation records of its generator that lie between that generator's previous part and itself in the file, and holds their lines exactly: byte for byte, in the order the records came. Where no record of its generator lies between, the part holds lines of its own. A re-cutter and tickwave bundle write a file's annotations that way, as parts and no annotation records, since a finished file needs none. A reader in order may check each part that covers records against them, as tickwave's does (see In order). A reader refuses a part with no line, and one with a line that has no start, a time that doesn't convert to ticks or an end before its start, since its row couldn't state the span, or that states a key twice. Repair and re-cutting take a generator's annotations from its parts and from its records after its last part, never from a record a part covers, so no annotation comes out twice. The annotation parts table (see Index) gives each part's place and the span its annotations cover, so a reader fetches only the parts a view reaches, and a long recording's annotations load as its data does.

A writer writes a generator's part once the lines since its last part reach a size it picks, after the record whose line takes them there. At the finish it writes each generator's rest as a part, the generators in the order of their first annotations. A re-cutter writes parts that stand alone the same way, as it reads the lines, and carries its input's bundle. A writer that bundles a sidecar's files given whole, as tickwave bundle does, refuses a generator file the hand-written file doesn't include, splits each generator file's lines into parts in the file's order, and writes them at its finish, the generators in the order the hand-written file includes their files.

The annotation bundle record holds the sidecar's files without the parts' lines. Its payload is a u32, the decoded length, then one zstd frame of UTF-8 JSON: an object of strings, each file's name, once, and its text, where a generator file's text is its first line alone, with its newline, and that line states no key twice. A reader refuses a bundle that names a file twice, or whose generator file is anything else. The whole generator file is that line, then the lines of every part whose generator is its own, in the order of the parts' rows. A live recording's hand-written file holds no annotation of its own, includes the generator files in the order of their generators' first annotations, and may hold a [view] (ANNOTATIONS.md, "The stored view"). A file holds at most one bundle record, written just before the footer, and the footer's top index places it. A recording with annotation parts has a bundle record, and each part's generator has a file in it; a reader refuses a recording that breaks either. A reader that wants the sidecar's files whole, to write them beside the recording, joins them back from the bundle and the parts. It leaves out and cuts annotations at the length as below while it joins, so the files it writes pass ANNOTATIONS.md's checks.

A reader leaves out an annotation that starts at or past the length, and ends a span that ends past it at the length. A generator gives each annotation an id of its own (ANNOTATIONS.md). tickwave's writer doesn't check that, since it would have to hold every id: its producer gives unique ids. A reader that meets a second annotation with an id its generator has given already keeps the one that comes first in the file's order, parts in the order of their rows and lines in order, and reports the other. A reader that fetches only the parts a view reaches can't see that a part it hasn't fetched repeats an id. No row indexes ids, so a reader resolves a hand-written annotation's ref once it has read every part.

Index#

Each channel's blocks, each level of a channel's overview in overview blocks, each gap or break list longer than 64 entries, and the recording's annotation parts are a table each: its entries in order. A table's entries go in pages of 4,096, the last page the rest, and the footer's top index lists the pages. Past 16 pages, directory pages list the pages, 4,096 entries a directory page, and the top index lists the directory pages; past 16 of those, another directory level goes above, and so on. So the top index holds at most 16 entries a table however long the recording, and a reader holds one page a level at a time.

Table Its entries Their count
a channel's blocks block rows the channel's blocks
one level of a channel's overview in overview blocks overview rows the level's rows
a digital channel's gaps, past 64 gaps gap_count
an analog channel's breaks, past 64 breaks break_count
the recording's annotation parts part rows annotation_parts

The top index lists only these tables. A table a later version adds keeps its pages in records of kinds from 128 up, and states where its top pages lie in a header field of that version. A reader in order refuses an index page or list page that no table's entries give.

A block row, 28 bytes:

Offset Type Field
0 i64 the block's start tick
8 u64 the offset of its zstd frame in the file
16 u32 the frame's length
20 u32 its decoded length
24 u32 the channel's word, as its block record has it

Block i of a channel covers ticks [start_i, start_{i+1}), and its last block runs to length. The first starts at tick 0, and the starts strictly increase. A reader refuses an index that places an edge or a sample outside its block.

An overview row, 20 bytes: its frame's offset (u64), the frame's length (u32), its decoded length (u32) and n (u32), as its overview block record has them. A level's rows go in order of k, each starting where the one before ends, and their n sum to the level's blocks. A reader refuses a zero n, rows whose n sum to another count, and a last row of more than one block.

A gap is 17 bytes, from (i64), to (i64) and level (u8); a break is 28 bytes, index (u64), tick (i64), lost (u64) and phase (u32), as Gaps defines them.

A part row, 32 bytes:

Offset Type Field
0 u64 the offset of its zstd frame in the file
8 u32 the frame's length
12 u32 its decoded length
16 i64 start, the earliest axis tick any of its annotations starts at
24 i64 end, the latest any of them ends at, a point ending where it starts

The rows go in the order the parts were written. A part's start and end say which views it can reach, so a reader fetches the parts whose span meets a view's: those with start before the view's end and end at or after its start. They lie in 0..2^53, start at most end, and may pass the length, since a live writer writes a part before it knows the length. The rows don't go in order of time, since a long span can start early in a late part, so the directory entries above them say which times each page reaches instead (below).

A page record's payload, for an index page (kind 10) and a list page (kind 11) alike:

Offset Type Field
0 u32 channel, a derived channel numbered after the channels; 0 for the annotation parts
4 u8 table: 0 blocks, 1 an overview level, 2 gaps, 3 breaks, 4 annotation parts
5 u8 the overview level's shift, 0 for any other table
6 u8 depth: 0 for a page of the table's entries, d for a directory of pages of depth d − 1
7 u64 its first entry's place among the entries at its depth, counted from 0
15 u32 the number of its entries, 1 to 4,096
19 the rest its entries

A list page holds gaps or breaks at depth 0; an index page holds block, overview or part rows at depth 0, or directory entries at any depth from 1. A directory entry, 28 bytes:

Offset Type Field
0 i64 first, of the first entry under it
8 u64 number, of the first entry under it
16 u64 the page record's offset in the file
24 u32 the page record's length, its framing included

first and number say where the page it points at starts: for blocks, the first block's start tick and its first item's index in the channel; for an overview level, the first row's k and its place among the rows; for gaps, the first gap's from and its place in the list; for breaks, the first break's tick and its index. For annotation parts they say which times the page reaches instead: first is the earliest start of the rows under it, and number the latest end, so a reader skips every page whose span misses a view's. The top index's entries are directory entries too.

A page record's length is 32 bytes plus its count of entries times their size: its table's own entry's at depth 0, and a directory entry's, 28 bytes, above. The table's shape gives each page's count (below), so a reader refuses a directory entry whose length differs from that before it fetches the page.

A table's shape follows from its count R. Its depth-0 pages are ceil(R / 4,096). Where the pages at a depth number at most 16, the top index lists them, and that depth is the table's. Otherwise directory pages one depth up list them, 4,096 entries a page in order, the last the rest. Counts are below 2^53, so a table has at most four directory levels: pages alone list 2^16 entries, one directory level 2^28, two 2^40, three 2^52, and a fourth every count past that. A reader refuses a page whose channel, table, shift or depth differ from the entry that points at it, whose first place or count differ from what the shape gives, or whose entries are out of order; a directory entry whose first or number differ from its page's; and a top index whose depth or entry count for a table differ from what the table's count gives.

A writer writes a table's page once it holds 4,096 entries, after the records its entries place, and a directory page once it holds 4,096 entries likewise. A page of gaps or breaks waits for the block that Gap and run records names. It writes every table's last pages at the finish.

8 + header_len index_len bytes the top index
8 + header_len + index_len the rest the strips: one zstd frame a channel

The top index:

Offset Type Field
0 u64 the annotation bundle record's offset in the file, 0 without one
8 u32 its length, its framing included, 0 without one
12 u8, u8, e entries where the header states annotation_parts, the annotation parts table: its depth, e (1 to 16), then e directory entries
after it the rest an entry for each channel, in channel order, then each derived channel

A channel's entry:

Type Field
u32 its strip frame's length
u32 the strip's decoded length
each of its tables in turn u8 depth, u8 e (1 to 16), then e directory entries

A channel's tables go in this order: its blocks; each level of its overview in overview blocks, finest first; and its gaps or breaks where the header states gap_count or break_count. A derived channel has only its overview levels. A reader refuses a top index that is not exactly index_len bytes.

The strips follow the top index, back to back in the order of its entries. A channel's strip is its coarsest overview level, all its buckets, laid out as the channel's level for them (see Overview). A reader refuses a strip whose decoded length is not that level's size, or whose frames don't end where the footer does. It decompresses only the strips of the channels it shows.

So the footer holds nothing that grows with the recording's length but the number of overview levels, which grows by one each time the length grows sixteenfold: the header without its long lists, at most 16 top-index entries a table, and a strip of at most 1,024 buckets a channel. A first draw reads the trailer and the footer and decompresses the strips it shows.

Content id#

The content id identifies what a recording holds, not how a file stores it. Re-cutting blocks, recompressing, re-indexing or bundling annotations leaves it unchanged. A sidecar pins it.

It is BLAKE3 over the timebase (two u64), the length (i64), each channel's own BLAKE3 digest, in channel order, and then each derived channel's digest, in order. A channel's digest covers what the declaration fixes, then the channel's items in order, then what only the end settles, so a writer computes it as the items arrive. A channel's gaps or breaks, as the header lists them, go into a digest of their own, and the channel's digest takes their count and that digest at the end, so a writer holds none of them to hash them, but the one it can't yet know is listed (see Gap and run records). Every tick in it is a source tick, as the channel stores it. Strings are written as a u64 byte length and their UTF-8 bytes.

Between what the declaration fixes and the items, a channel adds a byte of flags where any is set: 1 if it states offset, 2 if it states kept, 4 if the recording has several sources or the channel's source has an m other than 1 or an origin other than 0. Then offset as i64 and offset_kind as a string, where it states them; kept's from and to as i64, where it states it; and with flag 4, its source's m as two u64 in lowest terms, origin as i64, align as i64, 0 where the source states none, and stretch as two u64 in lowest terms, 1 and 1 where the source states none. A channel with no flag adds nothing, so a recording converted whole has the id its data alone gives.

  • Digital: the name, "digital", the initial level as one byte, then every edge's source tick as i64, then end as i64, the number of gaps as u64, and the gaps' digest: BLAKE3 over each gap's from and to as i64 and its level as one byte, in order.
  • Analog: the name, "analog", the unit, period as two u64 and phase as two u64, both in lowest terms (0 and 1 for no phase), then the samples, then first as i64, count as u64, the number of breaks as u64, and the breaks' digest: BLAKE3 over each break's index as u64, tick as i64, lost as u64 and phase as u32, in order. Where the channel states lost_before or lost_after, both follow as u64, 0 for one it doesn't state. The samples of an f32 or a codes channel are every value's f32 bits as u32, so the two codings of one capture's values give one id. An f64 channel adds "f64" before the flags and hashes every value's bits as u64. An int channel adds, in the same place, "int", bits as one byte, signed as one byte, and scale and offset as an i64 and a u64 each (1/1 and 0/1 where it states none), and hashes every code as i64 or u64.
  • Derived: the name, the unit, the op, the number of operands as u64, each operand's name in order, then the number of params as u64 and each one's key and value, in the byte order of the keys. All are strings, so a reader can compute a derived channel's part of the id whether or not it knows the operation. So changing an operation, or swapping the operands of a subtraction, changes the id, and so does editing the recipe an annotation pinned.

significant, overview_shift, codec, requires, and a source's fields other than the four flag 4 adds are not part of the content: they describe it, say how a file stores it, or follow from it. A source's timebase follows from its m, its stretch and the recording's timebase. Neither are auxiliary, auxiliary records, nor a field or record a later minor version adds: a reader of an earlier minor version computes the id without them, and a full check and re-cutting compare that id with the header's. A later version that makes a field or record content puts it behind requires. A channel of a kind a later version adds is content by that version's rule for the kind. A reader that doesn't know the kind can't compute its part, so it can't check the id (see Unavailable channels).

The header writes the id as blake3: and 64 lower-case hex digits, one spelling, as a sidecar compares it as text. A reader refuses any other.

Limits#

A writer stays within these, and a reader opens anything within them. A reader refuses a file past any of them, as an error it reports, before it fetches or allocates for the part that passes it:

  • A block decodes to at most 4 MiB, 2^22 bytes. So a block holds at most 2^22 edges, since each takes a byte at least, or 2^22 samples.
  • An overview block holds at most 4,096 buckets and a strip at most 1,024, at most 32 bytes a bucket (see Overview).
  • An annotation bundle decodes to at most 64 MiB, and an annotation part to at most 4 MiB. An annotation record's payload is at most 4 MiB too, so a part of its line alone fits. A recording has at most 256 generators. The annotations themselves have no limit: there can be as many parts as it takes.
  • A record of a kind this version defines is no longer than its kind holds. A gap record's payload is 21 bytes, a run record's 32 and a known-through record's 8, exactly. A block, overview block, annotation part or bundle record holds its fields and a frame within the bound below. A page is what its count gives. The declaration is at most 64 MiB, as the header is. A reader stops at a record whose n passes that before it reads the payload, as at one whose check doesn't match (see In order): what follows a crash can be any bytes. A block or overview block record of a channel it can't read is the exception (see Unavailable channels). A record of a kind from 128 up can be any length: a reader that skips one hashes it as it reads, without holding it.
  • The footer record is at most 256 MiB, its header at most 64 MiB of it. A recording within the other limits needs less: its top index and strips come to under 48 KiB a channel.
  • A recording has at most 4,096 channels and derived channels together.
  • A frame is no longer than zstd's bound for its decoded length, ZSTD_COMPRESSBOUND: no writer needs more, so a reader refuses a row or a record whose frame length passes it before fetching the frame.
  • A page's length is what its count gives (see Index).
  • An analog channel's grid D is below 2^16, its period at least 1, and a source's stretch lies in 1/2..=2 (see Header).

These bound what any one part of a file can make a reader decode or allocate, whatever the file states: a block's edges, held as 8-byte ticks, take at most 32 MiB. A reader on a smaller machine can hold less, and then reports each part it can't take as an error, rather than failing.

Writing#

A writer:

  • Writes the preamble and the declaration before anything else.
  • Takes each channel's items in the order of its source ticks. The channels keep no order among themselves.
  • Puts each item in its channel's open block, which starts where the channel's last block ended, at tick 0 for its first. No item of a channel lies before the start of its open block.
  • Closes a channel's block at an axis tick before which it has every item of the channel, and writes it.
  • Writes a gap or a run before the channel's block that needs it.
  • Writes annotation records as it generates them, and each generator's annotation part once its lines since the last one reach the size it picks (see Annotations).
  • Writes a known-through record at a tick before which every item of every channel is in a block record before it.
  • Writes overview blocks as their buckets settle. Block k of a channel's level settles once the channel is known past axis tick 4,096 (k + 1) × 2^shift: one of its blocks ends there or past it, or a known-through record's tick does. A run of alike blocks is written when it ends: when a block with other bytes settles, or at the finish.
  • Writes each table's pages as they fill (see Index).
  • At the end, closes every channel's last block at the length, then writes a known-through record at the length if it has written any (so a file cut off inside its end repairs to itself, but for what only the finish states: a source's extras at its end, losses after an analog channel's last sample, the generators' roles and a stored view; a loss after the last sample is content, so without it the repaired copy has another content id), each overview level's last block and its open run, each generator's last annotation part, every table's last pages, the annotation parts table's included, then the annotation bundle where it took any annotation or was given a stored view, the footer and the trailer.

A writer refuses, as an error, any tick, count or length that a reader would refuse, the axis tick an item maps to included, and anything past the limits (see Limits), rather than write a file no reader opens.

tickwave's writer#

Output. It hands its output to the caller as bytes, and counts every offset it writes, in rows and pages, from its first output byte, so the caller writes the output from the start of the file.

Order and promise. It takes each channel's items in that channel's order, and refuses an item before the start of its channel's open block. A promise, promise(before), says every item of every channel before axis tick before is in; promise_channel(c, before) says it of channel c alone. It refuses an item before the latest promise to its channel. A channel's open block closes once its items before the latest promise to it and to its partners reach the target size, the gaps it has taken that end before that tick counted at 17 bytes each: those items go in the closed block, and those at or after that tick stay in the open one. A derived channel's operands are partners: they close their blocks together, at one tick. So a producer can write a channel ahead of what it has promised, as a probe's samples come ahead of the pin edges that lie among them. Without a promise, a block closes only at a flush or the finish. Promises and flushes go forward: each is at or after the one before, so every block a writer closes before a flush ends at or before the flush's tick, which repair relies on. A promise to one channel can close its block ahead of the others, so the writer refuses a flush at a tick before the end of any block it has closed. The encoder, which takes items in axis order across channels, promises with each item, so its blocks close as they reach the target, and a sparse channel's one block at the finish.

Limits. Its target is at most 2 MiB, half the most a block can hold, 1 MiB unless it is given another. It refuses an item that would take a channel's open block past 4 MiB where the block can't close first: where closing at the latest promise to the channel and its partners would still leave too much, because the items lie at one axis tick or no promise has come past them. The producer then promises or flushes, and the item can go in. A flush always closes at its own tick, so a flush never meets this limit. It refuses a sample of a derived channel's operand that would leave more than 4 MiB of that operand's samples, in its coding, waiting for the other operand's: an operand can run ahead of its partner by at most that much.

Flush. flush(before) promises before, closes at before every channel's open block that holds an item, or the end of a gap it has taken, before it, and then writes one known-through record at before, after every block record that flush closed. Items at or after before stay in the open blocks. A channel with nothing before before in its open block writes no record; the known-through record says it holds nothing new before before. A flush at the latest known-through tick writes nothing. A live recorder flushes at least every second and syncs the file to disk after each, so how often it flushes, and the compression still pending, bound what a crash loses.

Compression. It compresses blocks at the level it is given, and overview blocks at zstd level 3, whatever that level is. Annotation parts are compressed at the level it is given, strips and the annotation bundle at the finish. By default the compression of every other frame is spread over the calls after its record is made, on the caller's thread: a frame is cut into slices of 8 KiB of decoded bytes from its start, and while any frame is pending, every call, take_output included, compresses the next slice, and the slices after it while they fit in 8 KiB together, so no call does much work however large a block is. Each slice ends a zstd block inside the frame, so the frame's bytes depend on the data, the level and the slice, never on how the calls fall. Its records leave in file order: a record waits behind the frames before it, so a flush's known-through record leaves only after the bytes of that flush's blocks. A closed block reaches the output within one call for each slice still to compress of the frames up to and including its own. A ready-made option compresses on other threads instead, channels in parallel, and gives the same bytes. finish compresses whatever is still pending.

Memory. It holds each channel's open block, at most 4 MiB, each table's open pages at each depth (at most 4,096 entries each) and the top index's entries, each overview level's open block and open run with their sums, each derived channel's waiting samples, at most 4 MiB, each generator's lines since its last annotation part, under 64 KiB and one line, each channel's gaps whose blocks are still open, at most 4 MiB of them, the frames waiting for compression, and the content id's running digests. Each of those is bounded whatever the recording's length, so a month-long recording holds a few open pages of index, not every block's row, gap or annotation. Some fill slowly: a coarse level's open block over days, a directory's open page over months, so the writer's memory rises toward that bound for a long while.

Gaps and runs. It writes a run's record with the run's first sample, so it leaves out no run. It hashes a gap, and adds it to its list's open page, once the channel's block that holds the gap's to closes. It refuses a gap that would leave more than 4 MiB of gaps waiting, at 17 bytes each, as it refuses an item past its limit; the producer then promises or flushes. At the finish it hashes a gap still waiting unless the channel's end leaves it out, and refuses an end that would leave out a gap it has hashed.

Annotations. It writes a generator's annotation part once that generator's lines since its last part reach 64 KiB, after the record whose line takes them there. Before a line that would take them past 4 MiB, it writes the lines it holds as a part first. At the finish it writes each generator's rest, then the bundle record, whose hand-written file includes the generator files. It doesn't check a generator's ids for duplicates. Nothing it does at the finish reads back what it wrote.

Reading#

With random access#

A reader reads the file's last 32 KiB first: over HTTP with Range: bytes=-32768, which a server answers with the whole file when it is shorter. It reads the trailer, and fetches the rest of the footer if the footer starts before those bytes. It parses the footer once, and decompresses the strips of the channels it shows: that is a first draw. For a span of a channel it then picks a level by the width of a column: the coarsest of the channel's levels whose bucket is no wider than a column, or the channel's items where even its finest is wider. It fetches that table's pages through the top index, directory pages first where the table has them, then the overview blocks or blocks the span covers, as the rows place them, and those either side of the span, so a pan seldom waits. It decodes each block once for a view, whatever channels the view takes from it. It checks each length a row or a directory entry states against the limits before it fetches (see Limits).

For annotations it reads the bundle record and the annotation parts table's rows, and fetches the parts whose span meets the view, as it fetches blocks. A reader that only draws the data may leave annotations until after the first draw.

Unavailable channels#

A channel is unavailable to a reader when its requires names a feature the reader doesn't know, its kind or its codec is one it doesn't know, or, for a derived channel, its op is one it doesn't know or an operand is unavailable. The reader still opens the recording. It keeps the channel's place and name, so every other channel keeps its number, and shows the channel as unavailable rather than leaving it out. It skips the channel's blocks, overview and strip; the top index still says where they are, so it reads past them. A later version can raise a limit for its own channels, so a reader holds an unavailable channel to the format's layout alone, not to the limits: its rows and lists to the shape and count of each page, row and entry, not to the values in them; its strip to the footer's bounds, not to a strip's size; and, reading in order, its block and overview block records to their framing alone, at any length a record holds, hashing and skipping each as it does a record of a kind from 128 up. It fetches and decodes none of the frames they place. It leaves out every annotation that names an unavailable channel in channels or measure, even one that names other channels too, and says which annotations it left out and why. An annotation whose channels is absent or empty names no channel by name, and stays.

A reader holds the rest of an unavailable channel's entry, where its kind is one it knows, to this version's rules all the same, its mapping into 0..length included (see Header). So a feature that changes how a source's ticks map to the axis goes in the requires of every channel of that source, and states an origin and a stretch that place each channel's kept span and end inside the axis by this version's rule too. A full check, which compares every item with what the file states, can't check a channel it can't read, so it refuses a recording that has one and says which.

In order#

A reader takes the records from the start. Each block record carries its channel, its span and its word, so the reader decodes a channel's blocks without the footer, and each known-through record extends every channel to its tick. It may skip pages, and annotation parts that cover records, whose contents follow from the records it reads; tickwave's reader checks each against them instead, and refuses one that differs by a byte from what the records before it give. A part that stands alone, and the annotation bundle, follow from no record: the bundle holds the hand-written file and each generator file's first line. The reader refuses each record that breaks a rule this document gives for records in order. It stops at the first record that runs past the end of the file, whose n passes its kind's limit, or whose check does not match, and keeps every record before it. A file that stops before its trailer is cut off, not finished: a full check refuses it, and repair can finish it. A file that ends with a trailer whose footer ends where the trailer starts is finished; if its records stop before that footer, it is damaged, not cut off. A reader with random access may still read it, and repair and re-cutting refuse it, since finishing it again would drop what follows the stop. After the footer record come exactly the trailer's 24 bytes.

A viewer reading a file that is still being written meets a last record cut off where the writer has got to. It reads that record again once the file grows. The declaration fixes each channel's finest level, so the viewer extends each channel's levels bucket by bucket as its blocks and the known-through records arrive, and adds a coarser level once the length needs one.

Repairing a cut-off file#

A cut-off file is repaired by appending what a finished file has after its last good record:

  1. Take the records up to the first bad one. Refuse a file with no declaration, or with no block, no known-through record and no annotation record.
  2. Where they hold a known-through record, cut the file just after the latest one: every channel is then known to its tick, and none holds more. Keep the annotation records that came after it, to write again after the cut. An annotation part after the cut that covers records goes, and its lines come back from the records; one that stands alone is kept, to write again. Note, too, each gap record after the cut whose from maps before the known-through tick: a loss the recorder had begun to report then ends its channel at that from (step 4), rather than being lost with the cut. It is written again only where its to maps at or past the length (step 3), as a gap the header leaves out (see Gap and run records); otherwise only its from stays. A record only a finish writes goes wherever it lies, since a file cut there was cut inside its finish and step 7 writes it again: a table's last page, one of fewer than 4,096 entries, and the annotation bundle. Where they hold none, each channel keeps its blocks to its last whole one, except that a derived channel's operands keep only the samples both hold: cut the file just after the last record at which every derived channel's operands hold as many samples, and keep the annotation records and parts after the cut as above.
  3. The length is the later of that known-through tick (or, with none, the latest end of any kept block, 0 with no block) and one past the latest start among the annotations kept, so what a recorder marked after its last data survives, and a recording of marks alone too. Each channel whose last kept block ends before the length gets an empty block from there, or from tick 0, to the length.
  4. Every digital channel's end is the source tick at the latest known-through tick, or, with none, at its own last kept block's end, or its start with no block, or the from of a gap noted in step 2 where that comes sooner. A file that can be repaired states no kept span, so its one source maps by T = k + origin, and that tick is the axis tick minus origin. No analog channel states a loss after its last sample.
  5. Count each channel's edges and samples from its blocks' words, and take its gaps and runs from their records.
  6. Compute each channel's overview and the content id from the kept blocks' items, decoding each block once. Keep the overview blocks and pages already written that the kept blocks give, and write the rest, the open runs included. Refuse a file whose overview blocks or pages differ from the ones its content gives. Each channel's levels are compared on their own, in order of k, whatever order the records of different channels and levels came in.
  7. Write the records kept in step 2 after the cut, in the order they came. Before a part that stands alone, write a part of the lines of its generator's records written again before it, so that it still stands alone. Then write what a writer's finish writes. For each generator, in the order of its first annotation, that is parts of the lines of its records after its last kept part, each within the limits. A record a kept part covers is not written into another part. Then come every table's last pages, the annotation bundle, the footer and the trailer.

A repaired file holds what a writer that had finished right after the records repair keeps, with the same length, would have written, and so has its content id.

A gap record among the records step 2 keeps is not written again but stays where it lies. Where one reaches its channel's end (step 4) while its to maps before the length (step 3), the header can't leave the gap out (see Gap and run records), and repair can't take the record out of what it keeps. Such a file is not repaired, but re-cut (see Re-cutting): the copy then holds the same content without the record. So is a file with an auxiliary record among the records step 2 keeps, since a tool that writes a recording again drops every one (see Preamble).

A file whose channels state kept, because a channel is moved or cut, the recording merges several sources, or its source states a stretch other than 1, is not repaired. Its declaration states each channel's kept span before any data, to the end of the whole capture, so a footer at an earlier tick would contradict it. Such a file is made again from its sources instead.

Re-cutting#

A file whose blocks a recorder closed early, at flushes, can be written again with blocks of the usual size, and a repaired file too. A re-cutter reads the records in order and writes the same declaration, then every channel's items, and the gaps and runs its header lists, in the same order, and finishes with the same length, ends, losses, extras and annotations. It writes the annotations as parts that stand alone, with no annotation records (see Annotations). It may give the channels other overview shifts than the input declares, such as the ones the rule gives each channel's own items; a finished input's header says enough to work them out. It computes the overview again. The new file has the same content id. A cut-off file re-cuts as repair would finish it, and so does one repair refuses for a gap record it keeps: a re-cutter leaves out every gap record that reaches its channel's end while its to maps before the length, wherever it lies, and ends the channel at the earliest such gap's from.
